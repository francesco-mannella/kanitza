# Mini-Review: Topographic Population Codes and Generalization by Interpolation

## Why this matters for the project

Section 5.2.2 of `draft.tex` (`subsec:bio-separation-protection`) argues that Topographic Gating needs a continuous grid, not a hard partition, because continuity between committed anchor positions is what licenses interpolation-based generalization: generative replay and reconstruction at grid coordinates never directly trained both depend on the decoder producing a sensible output at points between the ones actually seen during training. The draft currently backs this claim with a single citation, Georgopoulos, Schwartz and Kettner's 1986 motor-cortex population-vector study. That paper is the right classic reference but it is one data point from one system (arm-movement direction coding) reported forty years ago. This review surveys the broader and more recent biological literature that supports the same structural claim: that a topographically or continuously organized neural population code interpolates smoothly across the space it tiles, and that this property is used by the brain for generalization to conditions never directly experienced.

## Classic foundations (population vectors)

Georgopoulos, Schwartz and Kettner (1986) showed that a population vector read out from directionally tuned motor cortical neurons, each with a preferred direction and a cosine-shaped tuning curve, reconstructs the direction of an arm movement smoothly across the full range of directions, including directions that individual neurons in the population were never maximally tuned to. The output is a weighted average over the population's preferred directions, i.e. an interpolation.

Zemel, Dayan and Pouget (1998) generalized the population-vector idea into a probabilistic framework in which a population code represents an entire probability distribution over a stimulus variable rather than a single point estimate, still built on smooth, overlapping tuning curves as the code's basis functions. Pouget, Dayan and Zemel's subsequent review (2000, *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*) makes the interpolation argument explicit and general: homogeneous population codes, because of their formal relationship to basis-function approximation schemes, support nonlinear computations, including generalization to inputs between the ones sampled by the population's tuning curves. This is the direct theoretical grounding for treating "population code over smoothly tuned units" and "basis-function interpolation" as the same object, which is the move this project's grid readout also makes.

## Topographic maps and smooth generalization

Beyond single-variable motor coding, topographic organization is a general property of primary sensory cortex, and its geometry is treated as smooth and continuous by the methods used to measure it. In visual cortex, retinotopic mapping explicitly assumes that true retinotopic organization is continuous, so that discontinuities observed empirically are attributed to measurement noise rather than to genuine map structure; recent work on quantifying cortical magnification (the local scaling between visual field position and cortical surface area) uses topology-preserving smoothing and optimal transport specifically to recover this assumed-continuous structure from noisy fMRI-derived maps. This is indirect evidence for the same point argued here on different grounds: the object being modeled is treated by the field as a smooth interpolating function over cortical position, not a lookup table of independently measured points.

Whether this continuity is functionally necessary or a side effect of a separate constraint (minimizing the length of neuronal wiring) is an open and unresolved debate discussed under Open Questions below; either way, the empirical maps themselves are smooth, and models of how they support downstream computation, including this paper's own, standardly treat that smoothness as usable structure rather than incidental packaging.

## Cognitive maps and neural manifold geometry

A more recent and more direct line of evidence comes from the neural-manifold literature, which asks not just whether a map is smooth in physical cortical space, but whether the geometry of population activity itself supports generalization.

Gardner, Hermansen, Pachitariu, Burak, Baas, Dunn, Moser and Moser (2022, *Nature*) recorded from hundreds of grid cells simultaneously and showed, using topological data analysis, that the joint population activity of a grid cell module lies on a toroidal manifold, exactly as predicted by continuous attractor network models. Positions on this torus correspond to positions in physical space, and the same toroidal structure is preserved across different environments and even during sleep. This is a direct empirical demonstration that a spatial population code is organized as a continuous, topologically constrained manifold rather than as a set of independently tunable units, which is the structural precondition for the code to interpolate at all.

Bernardi, Benna, Rigotti, Munuera, Fusi and Salzman (2020, *Cell*) introduced the "geometry of abstraction" framework, defining abstraction operationally as the ability of a linear decoder trained on some task conditions to generalize to conditions it was not trained on, and showing that hippocampal and prefrontal population activity adopts geometries (low-dimensional, structured subspaces for abstract variables, alongside high-dimensional, flexible geometry for task-specific variables) that support exactly this kind of generalization. Tang, Shin and Jadhav (2023, *Cell Reports*) extended this to a behaving-animal, cross-region comparison: as rats generalize a navigational rule across physically distinct environments, hippocampal maps stay environment-specific while prefrontal maps undergo a geometric transformation into an abstract, environment-invariant format that generalizes to novel spatial contexts. Together these two papers make the manifold-geometry version of the interpolation claim: generalization to untrained conditions is not a side effect of having a population code at all, but a property of specific geometric arrangements of that code, and the brain appears to construct such arrangements deliberately in circuits (prefrontal cortex) whose job includes generalizing.

## Computational models

On the modeling side, several literatures independently converge on the claim that imposing topographic (spatially continuous) structure on a population of units, rather than leaving them independently tunable, is what buys interpolation and generalization as opposed to memorization.

The self-organizing map itself is the oldest example: a trained SOM is described in the standard literature as topology-preserving specifically because nearby prototype vectors on the grid are close in input space, which is what allows the map to characterize inputs it was never trained on by assimilating them to the nearest, and to interpolate between neighboring prototypes rather than only reproducing stored ones exactly. This is the same property Section 2.1 of `draft.tex` calls "soft queryability."

Doshi and Konkle (2023, *Science Advances*) trained a self-organizing map over the representational space of a deep neural network's object embeddings and found that the resulting topography reproduces multiple brain-like organizational motifs of human ventral visual cortex (large-scale organization by animacy and object size, with face- and scene-selective regions emerging without being built in), evidence that a topographic constraint applied to a rich representational space, not only to raw pixels or coordinates, reproduces cortex-like organization as an emergent consequence rather than a design choice.

Deb, Deb and Murty's TopoNets (2025, ICLR, arXiv:2501.16396) go a step further computationally: a topographic loss applied to standard vision and language backbones (ResNet, ViT, GPT-Neo) produces models that are simultaneously the best-performing supervised topographic models to date and that reproduce topographic signatures found in visual and language cortex, without materially sacrificing task performance. Truong and Hasson (2025, arXiv:2508.00043) directly test what the added topographic constraint buys computationally in a CNN, comparing two local spatial-smoothness losses against matched non-topographic controls, and report that topographic regularization improves robustness to input perturbation and reshapes unit tuning, a machine-learning-side demonstration that the constraint is not merely decorative. These 2023 to 2025 results are the most current, most directly relevant computational precedent for this project's own claim, since they impose topography on exactly the kind of rich, learned representational space this paper's decoder occupies, rather than on a low-dimensional physical variable like direction or retinal position.

## Open questions

Two caveats limit how strongly this literature should be read as licensing the paper's claim.

First, there is a live, unresolved debate over whether cortical topography is functionally necessary for computation at all, or is instead a byproduct of a separate constraint, minimizing the total length of neuronal wiring, that would arise even if the represented variable and topographic position had no functional relationship. Under a pure wiring-length account, a thought experiment sometimes invoked in this literature notes that scrambling the physical positions of neurons while preserving their connections would leave the represented function unchanged, meaning topography per se would be doing no computational work; a contrasting view holds that topography reflects, and thereby exploits, statistical structure already present in the input (redundancy and correlation) and is not reducible to wiring cost alone. This paper's claim survives either way, since it is a claim about the decoder's own continuity being useful for interpolation, not a claim that cortex uses topography for this specific reason, but the biological analogy is weaker if topography's primary role in cortex turns out to be wiring economy rather than generalization.

Second, "interpolation" is a convenient computational description of what a population code or topographic map does, not necessarily a literal claim about the underlying biological mechanism. Population-vector decoding, basis-function population codes, and manifold-geometry generalization are all mathematically interpolation-like, but whether the same word describes one unified biological mechanism across motor cortex, grid cells, and prefrontal abstraction, or several different mechanisms that happen to produce interpolation-like behavior as an emergent property, is not settled by any single paper surveyed here.
