# Topographic maps as a general principle of brain organization: a minireview

## 1. A general principle

Topographic maps, in which neighboring neurons represent neighboring values of a stimulus or response dimension, are among the most consistent features of the nervous system. Kaas (1997) observed that in all mammals much of the neocortex consists of orderly representations of receptor surfaces, topographic at the global level and modular at the local level, and argued that such maps are adaptive: they allow interconnections between appropriate sets of neurons to be made efficiently and provide a suitable substrate for common spatiotemporal computations. From a comparative perspective, the neocortex of every mammal is composed of cortical fields whose organization and connections vary with the animal's ecological niche, and the mechanisms that generate these fields have been modified across lineages to produce that variability (Krubitzer, 2007). Arcaro and Livingstone (2024) push the idea further, arguing that the most fundamental innate organization of cortex in general, not just of the visual pathway, is a map-based topography that governs how the environment maps onto the brain, how brain areas interconnect, and how the brain processes information, with specialized domains emerging from its interplay with experience.

## 2. Sensory cortex

The classical evidence comes from the primary sensory systems. In the somatosensory system, Mountcastle (1957) characterized the modality and topographic properties of single neurons in cat somatic sensory cortex. In vision, 25 topographic maps have been identified in a large population of human subjects (N = 53) and combined into probabilistic atlases of visual cortex (Wang et al., 2015). In audition, fMRI responses to natural sounds reveal two mirror-symmetric frequency gradients on Heschl's gyrus, and tonotopic organization extends beyond the primary areas into higher-order and category-selective regions, with voice- and speech-selective regions occupying the low-frequency portions of the map (Moerel et al., 2012).

## 3. Motor cortex

Motor and premotor cortex are also topographic, and this organization is present very early. In newborn macaques, the somatomotor system spanning frontoparietal cortex and subcortex already contains multiple topographic body representations, indistinguishable at the large scale from those of older monkeys; finer differentiation of individual fingers increases over the first two years, and the maps are unchanged in visually impaired monkeys who rely on touch (Arcaro et al., 2019). In human preterm infants (31 to 36 weeks postmenstrual age), somatosensory stimulation of wrists, ankles and mouth already produces the spatial layout of the mature homunculus in primary sensorimotor cortex (Dall'Orso et al., 2018). Topography in motor areas is not restricted to the body: in the human supplementary motor area, neural populations tuned to different durations are orderly mapped in contiguous portions of cortex to form chronomaps, where each portion is enhanced by neighboring durations and suppressed by distant ones (Protopapa et al., 2019).

## 4. Associative cortex

Topographic maps extend well beyond primary areas. Retinotopic mapping combined with attention, working memory and saccade tasks has revealed multiple areas in human parietal and frontal cortex, each containing a topographic map of visual space (Silver and Kastner, 2009). In frontoparietal cortex these maps represent both polar angle and eccentricity and are organized into clusters that share a confluent fovea, as in visual cortex (Mackey et al., 2017). Along the auditory and language pathway, temporal receptive windows form a hierarchical topography, from early auditory cortex, driven by momentary input, to parietal and frontal areas at the apex that respond reliably only to intact paragraphs (Lerner et al., 2011).

## 5. Continuous gradients rather than discrete areas

Topography does not require sharply bounded, modality-specific maps. Arcaro and Livingstone (2021) argue that category-selective modules in the ventral stream are not innate specializations but reflect the effect of experience on a domain-general architecture in which topographic maps are fundamental. Consistent with this, large parts of macaque inferotemporal cortex are organized as a coarse map of a low-dimensional object space, in which category-selective regions are embedded and which repeats across three hierarchical stages (Bao et al., 2020). In human occipitotemporal cortex, object responses are organized medial to lateral by real-world size (Konkle and Oliva, 2012), and jointly by animacy and size into a spoked, tripartite arrangement around the occipital pole (Konkle and Caramazza, 2013). At the scale of the whole cortex, a principal gradient of connectivity runs from primary sensory and motor regions to transmodal regions of the default-mode network, in both humans and macaques (Margulies et al., 2016), and has been proposed as a core organizing axis and intrinsic coordinate system of human cortex (Huntenburg et al., 2018).

## 6. Non-spatial features: numerosity and meaning

The organizing dimension of a map need not be a sensory surface. Neural populations in human parietal cortex tuned to small numerosities are organized topographically, forming a numerosity map robust to low-level stimulus changes, which extends topographic principles to higher-order abstract features in association cortex (Harvey et al., 2013). Object size is also mapped topographically in posterior parietal cortex; its tuning properties differ from those of numerosity, but the two maps largely overlap and their preferences correlate, and neither reflects the structure of a sensory organ (Harvey et al., 2015). Semantic content is mapped as well: voxel-wise modeling of responses to hours of narrative speech reveals intricate semantic maps across cortex that are consistent across individuals (Huth et al., 2016). Semantic maps evoked by silent movies in visual cortex and by stories immediately anterior to it correspond along the border of visual cortex, suggesting that the two networks are smoothly joined into one contiguous map (Popham et al., 2021).

## 7. Cross-modal alignment of maps

When maps from different modalities converge, they are brought into register. The superior colliculus is the best studied case. In the newborn rhesus monkey, many deep-layer collicular neurons are already multisensory, their receptive fields are ordered into well-organized topographic maps, and the different receptive fields of the same multisensory neuron show a good degree of cross-modal spatial register; yet these neurons cannot yet synthesize their multisensory inputs (Wallace and Stein, 2001). Registration is partly established by activity-independent mechanisms: in mice, the topography of the projection from primary somatosensory cortex to the colliculus forms in the first postnatal week independently of retinal input, depends on graded ephrin-A expression, and aligns with visual inputs through a gradient-matching mechanism (Triplett et al., 2012). Integration, in contrast, depends on experience: animals raised without visual cues develop many multisensory collicular neurons that respond to each modality alone but cannot integrate them (Wallace et al., 2004). More generally, topographic maps remain plastic throughout life, and spatiotemporal patterns of activity provide instructive cues that organize visual projections (Cline et al., 2023). Popham et al. (2021) provide a cortical analogue of cross-modal registration: semantic maps from vision and language meet and align along a shared border.

## 8. Relevance for topological alignment

Taken together, these findings support three premises of topological alignment. First, topographic organization is a general-purpose representational format, reused across sensory, motor and associative domains. Second, the dimensions organized topographically include abstract, non-spatial ones such as numerosity, duration and meaning. Third, maps from different modalities are brought into register, partly through built-in mechanisms and partly through experience. Topological alignment proposes that this registration is driven by the agent's own actions, via a shared anchor given by the map position of the chosen action.
