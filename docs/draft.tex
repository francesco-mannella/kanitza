\begin{abstract}

\normalsize
Our eyes are always moving, constantly shifting what we see on our retina.
Nevertheless we perceive a seamless visual world.

Two main hypotheses explain how we perceive a stable visual world despite constant eye movements: retinotopic updating and spatiotopic updating.
Retinotopic updating suggests that our brains continuously adjust our perception based on changes happening directly on the retina.
Spatiotopic updating also proposes that our brains build a stable, "world-centered" map of our surroundings, keeping our perception consistent regardless of where our eyes are looking.
However, recent evidence increasingly favors the retinotopic hypothesis over the spatiotopic one.
Starting from the retinotopic hypothesis, but without shifting receptive fields, we propose an ideomotor explanation wherein visual stability is predicated on learned, aligned action-perception interactions.
This framework integrates saccades and pre- and post-saccadic retinal states into an ideomotor schema, conceptualizing them as actions, conditions, and effects.
Retinal states select saccades and address the effects those saccades are expected to produce; upon achievement, these effects become the conditions for subsequent saccades.
Acquired ideomotor chains reflect the affordances inherent within experienced visual scenes.
Stable perception is hypothesised to rest on the congruence between saccades and visual outcomes, learned during experience as an alignment of motor commands and resultant sensory inputs that reflects the interactive possibilities within the visual environment.
Critically, feedback correction is not necessary for this process.
We hypothesize that this ideomotor schema is neurally instantiated through the alignment of brain regions governing eye movements with those processing visual information, forming an ideomotor internal space.
Within this neural space, an action, its prompting conditions, and its anticipated visual outcome converge into a unique neural assembly spanning the eye-movement and visual regions.
These neural assemblies represent specific prototypes of retino-saccadic contingency patterns.
Similar contingency patterns are clustered within the space, such that both the saccadic and visual neural components maintain a common topology.
To demonstrate the functional validity of this mechanism, we present a computational model of voluntary saccades operating within simplified simulated visual environments.
This model reproduces key features of voluntary oculomotor behavior and offers a functional reading of the influence of the fronto-parietal network, specifically the frontal eye fields (FEF) and the lateral intraparietal cortex (LIP), on the superior colliculus (SC) in humans and primates. It gives the functional counterpart of remapping, a learned relation between a view, a saccade and its expected effect, and not its neural signatures: no receptive field shifts, and stability across different starting views is yet to be tested.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\paragraph{Visual perception is based on saccades.} 
Our brains do not passively record a continuous ``video'' of what we see.
Instead, we actively build our visual experience from snippets of information gathered through rapid eye movements.
Imagine you're looking at a painting in a museum: you don't just stare blankly at the center.
Instead, your eyes are constantly jumping from one detail to another – the artist's signature in the corner, a particular face in a crowd scene, a splash of color in the background.
Each of these jumps is a saccade.
Each time your eyes land on a new point, the light reflecting off that part of the painting stimulates your retina, sending a new burst of information to your brain.
You don't consciously perceive the gaps or the blurriness that occur during those saccades \citep{bridgemanFailureDetectDisplacement1975,burrSelectiveSuppressionMagnocellular1994,bindaVisionSaccadicEye2018}.
Why? Does your brain cleverly stitch together snapshots to create a complete, detailed picture? Does it generate missing visual information, simulating a ``video'' recording?  This is the explanation preferred by one prevalent and widely accepted theory, which posits that our brains actively compensate for the constant shifts in our gaze \citep{sommerWhatBrainStem2004,sunCorollaryDischargeOculomotor2016, cavanaughSaccadicCorollaryDischarge2016}.
This theory suggests that we don't just receive visual information; instead, our visual system uses predictions generated by an internal model based on our voluntary eye movements to correct and stabilize the incoming visual input.
Essentially, as our eyes move, our brain uses the information about these movements and adjusts what we perceive, allowing us to experience a stable and continuous visual world even though the raw retinal image is constantly changing.

\paragraph{Spatiotopic vs retinotopic hypotheses}

The standard retinotopic hypothesis assumes that a retinotopic spatial representation is updated in order to compensate for retinal displacement.
In this view, predictive remapping of receptive fields (Duhamel et al 1992) or attentional pointers (Cavanagh et al 2010) keeps track of the location of objects in retinotopic space.
 

Here we propose instead that a single location of the aligned space is at once a condition, an effect and an action.
When a retinal state activates its location, the activity of that location pre-activates, in a generative way, two retinal regions: the region of the current stimulus (the condition) and the region where the stimulus will fall after the saccade (the effect).
The same activity selects the saccade; executing it ends the decision and resets the mechanism, and the corollary discharge is hypothesised to be the signal that resets it.
Nothing shifts. What looks like remapping, a cell responding at its future field before the saccade and at its current field after it, is the pairing of two stable retinal regions in one location, learned from experienced saccades.
We claim that this is a possible alternative to the shifting of receptive fields, not the only one, and that it can be implemented at the neural level.

The ability to foveate the target despite changes in the starting condition of foveal stimulation is considered an operational definition of stability in spatial perception (Cf Rao et al 2016).


 
\paragraph{Topological Alignment hypothesis.} This hypothesis explains how goals guide the selection of saccadic eye movements within an ideomotor framework.
Its core idea is that we learn an internal neural space made of aligned maps where each aligned subregion encodes: a prototypical retinal state during fixation, a prototypical retinal state representing the visual effect of a saccade, and the motor command for that saccade.
This internal space is learned through an open-ended process guided by the  offered by experienced visual environments.
During this learning, bottom-up salience guides the formation of connections between specific visual events, the subsequent saccadic movements, and the resulting retinal activities after the saccades.
The topological nature of this internal representation enables generalization and interpolation across various sensory and motor options.
As a result this topological neural system approximates a continuous map of all possible contingencies between oculomotor movements and retinal effects.

\section{Visual stability across saccades: accounts and neural substrates}
\label{sec:substrates}

\subsection{The problem and the machinery shared by the accounts}

Each saccade, typically every 200 to 300 ms, moves every object to a new retinal location.
The early visual system is retinotopic, whereas perception is largely world-centred \citep{fabiusTimeCourseSpatiotopic2019,fairhallSpatiotopicUpdatingSaccades2017}.
Two lines of explanation structure the field and a third deflates the problem.
They differ in the frame of the representation, in what the eye-movement signal is used for, and in what a neuron or a percept does when a saccade puts the frames in opposition.

Both lines rely on a copy of the saccade command, the corollary discharge (CD).
Eye-position information survives the loss of extraocular proprioception, so it is internal and not reafferent \citep{guthrieCorollaryDischargeProvides1983}.
In the monkey the copy runs from the superior colliculus (SC) through the mediodorsal thalamus (MD) to the frontal eye field (FEF) \citep{sommerPathwayPrimateBrain2002,sommerWhatBrainStem2004,sommerInfluenceThalamusSpatial2006}; reviews are \citet{crapseCorollaryDischargeAnimal2008} and \citet{sunCorollaryDischargeOculomotor2016}.
Sensitivity falls around saccades and perisaccadic stimuli are mislocalised toward the target; suppression hides the smear but does not by itself restore position \citep{rossChangesVisualPerception2001,bindaVisionSaccadicEye2018}.
Most remapping data use a voluntary, planned saccade whose command is available before the eye moves, which is what lets the CD act predictively.
The open question is what the CD does downstream: shift receptive fields, update a world-centred frame, or only correct the visual estimate.

\subsection{Spatiotopic representations}

The spatiotopic account holds that position is coded in head- or world-centred coordinates, so stability is a property of the map.
The substrate is the gain field: the angle of gaze modulates, in a graded way, the responses of parietal neurons whose receptive fields are retinotopic, so that a population could read out head-centred position \citep{andersenInfluenceAngleGaze1983}.
Area VIP contains receptive fields on a continuum from eye- to head-centred \citep{duhamelSpatialInvarianceVisual1997}, and MST neurons signal target motion in world coordinates \citep{ilgPosteriorParietalCortex2004}.
In humans the positional motion aftereffect is selective in external coordinates after a saccade while the classic aftereffect stays retinotopic \citep{turiSpatiotopicPerceptualMaps2012}, repetition suppression in parietal cortex and FEF is spatiotopic \citep{fairhallSpatiotopicUpdatingSaccades2017}, and spatiotopic updating can appear within 150 ms \citep{fabiusTimeCourseSpatiotopic2019}.
The critiques are that only a minority of neurons is spatiotopic, that post-saccadic eye-position signals are unreliable and slow \citep{xuPostsaccadicUnreliabilityGain2012,morrisDynamicsEyepositionSignals2012}, that behavioural effects take hundreds of milliseconds to build \citep{zimmermannSpatiotopicNeuralRepresentations2013}, and that higher visual cortex codes location retinotopically \citep{golombHigherLevelVisual2012}.
The timing critique is answered by \citet{fabiusTimeCourseSpatiotopic2019}.

\subsection{Retinotopic remapping}

The remapping account keeps everything eye-centred and uses the CD to shift activity to where the stimulus will land, so no explicit world map is needed.
In monkey LIP, neurons respond to a stimulus placed where their receptive field will be after the saccade, before the eye moves, and to the trace of a stimulus that was extinguished before the saccade \citep{duhamelUpdatingRepresentationVisual1992}; LIP is read as a salience map updated by the intended movement \citep{colbySpaceAttentionParietal1999}.
In FEF the remapping depends on the collicular, thalamic CD pathway, as shown by inactivating the thalamus \citep{sommerInfluenceThalamusSpatial2006}.
Remapping is found in V3A (52\% of neurons), V3 and V2 and is very rare in V1 \citep{nakamuraUpdatingVisualRepresentation2002}, and in V4 it comes in two types, a response at the future field and a response at the saccade target \citep{neupaneTwoDistinctTypes2016}.
Behaviourally, attention shifts predictively to the post-saccadic retinotopic location \citep{rolfsPredictiveRemappingAttention2011}, and a handful of attention pointers, not a map, may be what is updated \citep{cavanaghVisualStabilityBased2010}.
The critiques are that in later FEF data receptive fields converge on the saccade target instead of shifting forward \citep{zirnsakVisualSpaceCompressed2014}, and that remapping is transient, so it does not by itself produce persistent world-centred perception.

\subsection{Reconciliation, the deflationary view and what stays open}

The two lines now look complementary more than rival: fast, predictive, retinotopic remapping covers the saccade itself, and a slower, sparser spatiotopic signal builds afterwards.
Timing helps to reconcile them, since spatiotopic effects were measured as slow until a continuous gaze-contingent probe found them within 150 ms, so the paradigm, not only the brain, set the estimate.
Spatiotopy may also be partial, and the task and the stimulus seem to decide which frame wins.
A deflationary view holds that little is carried across saccades and the world serves as its own memory: displacements go undetected during saccades \citep{bridgemanFailureDetectDisplacement1975}, blanking the target restores detection \citep{deubelPostsaccadicTargetBlanking1996}, and pre- and post-saccadic information are combined statistically \citep{niemeierOptimalTranssaccadicIntegration2003}; vision is then the mastery of sensorimotor contingencies \citep{oreganSensorimotorAccountVision2001} with a small transsaccadic memory \citep{irwinInformationIntegrationSaccadic1991}.
This view does not explain predictive remapping neurons or fast spatiotopic effects.

Three points stay open and bear on what follows.
First, whether remapping carries feature content or only location.
Transsaccadic predictions are learned from experience and bias peripheral perception toward the feature that the saccade has previously brought to the fovea \citep{herwigWhenCirclesBecome2015}, which a pure location shift does not predict.
Second, how the world-centred signal is read out.
Third, how single-neuron remapping, which differs among areas and in the later data converges on the target, relates to the perceptual stability that it is supposed to support.
The proposal of the next section starts from the retinotopic line but replaces the shift of receptive fields by a learned relation among a view, a saccade and its effect.

\section{Topological Alignment}
\label{sec:ta}

Topological Alignment (TA) is the hypothesis that visual stability and the guidance of saccades rest on one learned structure: a set of maps, one for the retinal view before a saccade, one for the saccade, one for the retinal view after it, that share a common topology and are aligned location by location.
We state it at three levels: what it does, how it could be implemented in the brain, and how it is formalised in the model of the next section.

\subsection{Functional level: a location is a condition, an action and an effect}

A location of the aligned space is at once the condition of a saccade, the saccade, and its effect.
A retinal state activates a location; the activity of that location pre-activates the retinal region of the current stimulus (the condition) and the retinal region where the stimulus is expected to fall after the saccade (the effect), and selects the saccade.
Anticipation is therefore an address, not a computation: the expected effect is the content stored at the location that the current view selects, and no module computes where an item will land.
Executing the saccade ends the decision and resets the mechanism; the corollary discharge is hypothesised to be the reset signal.
Once the saccade is made, the effect becomes the condition of the next one, so that experience is organised in chains of conditions, actions and effects, the ideomotor chains of the abstract.

Stability is the persistence of the neighbourhood relation between these three terms.
A view, the saccade selected by it and the view that follows keep addressing the same location, or locations that are close to each other, across experiences.
The relation is learned from the saccades that the agent itself makes.
Bottom-up salience drives exploration at first; the agent's competence, the degree to which the three maps agree at a location, then gates how much exploration is left and how plastic the location remains.
Goals (the locations selected by the conditions map) follow from the offered views and from the history of the episode, not from a position in external space.

\subsection{Neural level: aligned maps with a common topology}

We read the three maps as populations in the fronto-parietal network that governs saccades: the saccade map in the frontal eye fields (FEF) and, downstream, the superior colliculus (SC), the condition and effect maps in the lateral intraparietal cortex (LIP) and visual areas.
The alignment is the correspondence between neighbouring units of the three populations.
A neural assembly spanning them is a prototype of a retino-saccadic contingency, and neighbouring assemblies hold similar contingencies.
This reading is a hypothesis; the model does not simulate these areas, and the identification of the maps with FEF and LIP follows the functional correspondence only.

Two consequences follow for how such a system looks to an experimenter.
First, spatial order in a map does not show that a coordinate system is held and updated.
The maps are organised by the global content of the views and by their alignment with the saccade map, not by position, yet retinotopy appears in them as a by-product: position is one dimension of retina-centred input, and a saccade is a retinal vector, so a map aligned with the saccade map inherits order in retinal displacement.
We call this retinotopy indirect.
Second, what looks like remapping, a cell responding at its future field before the saccade and at its current field after it, can result from the pairing of two stable retinal regions at one location, without any receptive field shifting.
We claim this as a possible alternative to the shifting of receptive fields, not as the only one.
It predicts that (i) the layout of a map follows the statistics of the views it is trained on, (ii) the effect region of a location follows the landing place of the stimulus that activates the location, and this relation is reduced if the pairing between conditions and effects is broken in training, and (iii) lesions of a neighbourhood displace a goal to neighbouring locations rather than to a wrong coordinate.
Section~\ref{sec:model} tests (ii); (i) and (iii) are not tested.

\subsection{Formal level: a lattice of aligned prototypes}
\label{sec:formal}

Let a lattice of $N$ locations carry three maps with prototypes $w^{c}_j$ (condition), $w^{a}_j$ (action) and $w^{e}_j$ (effect), $j=1,\dots,N$.
For a view $x$, the goal is the winner of the conditions map,
\begin{equation}
g(x)=\arg\min_j \lVert x-w^{c}_j\rVert^2 ,
\end{equation}
the saccade is the action prototype $w^{a}_{g}$ and the expected effect is $w^{e}_{g}$.
A location is stable for an experienced triple (view $x$, action $a$, post-saccadic view $y$) when the winners of the action and effects maps for $a$ and $y$ are close to $g(x)$ on the lattice,
\begin{equation}
\mu=\tfrac12\sum_{m\in\{a,e\}}\exp\!\left(-(d_m/\sigma_\mu)^2\right),
\end{equation}
with $d_m$ the lattice distance between $g(x)$ and the winner of map $m$.
The maps are trained so that this holds, with the supervised topological map loss
\begin{equation}
L=\tfrac12\sum_j \lVert x-w_j\rVert^2\,\phi_j\,\psi_j ,
\end{equation}
where $\phi_j$ is a Gaussian neighbourhood of the map's own winner, which gives each map its topology, and $\psi_j$ is a Gaussian of width $\sigma_t$ around the goal $g$, which is the anchor that aligns the three maps.
The anchor resembles an efference signal: it is written by the selected action and aligns the sensory maps to it.
It is an address on a shared lattice and carries no forward model of the image or of space.

\subsection{The model}

\subsubsection{Architecture}

An agent explores a simple coloured object, a triangle or a square, in a simulated $80\times80$ task space.
The agent sees an $80\times80$ retinal window centred on its gaze and a central fovea; the action is a displacement of the gaze.
A bottom-up saliency front end (Gabor filters on colour-opponent channels) turns the retinal image into a saliency map, which a Gaussian attentional mask centred on a retinal point multiplies; the next gaze position is the maximum of the result.
The offline controller holds three topological maps on a common $10\times10$ lattice: the visual conditions map (the foveal colour-saliency view before a saccade, $16\times16$ pixels), the attention map (the retina-normalised centre of the attentional mask that produced the saccade) and the visual effects map (the foveal view after it).
The winner of the conditions map for the current view is the goal; the saccade is the attention prototype at the goal.
Position in the external world is not available to the controller, and neither is eye position: the fovea is retina-centred and the action is a retinal vector.

\subsubsection{Visual system, salience encoding and attentional filtering}
\label{sec:visual-system}

\paragraph{Retina and fovea.}
The agent sees an $80\times80$ pixel retinal image centred on its gaze; the objects are filled red or blue shapes on a white background.
The visual input of the maps is not the image but the fovea: the colour saliency of the central $64\times64$ pixels, resized to $16\times16$ (Figure~\ref{fig:visual-system}f).

\paragraph{Salience encoding.}
The retina is projected on eight colour masks (red minus green, green minus red, blue minus green, green minus blue, blue minus red, red minus blue, brightness and inverted brightness) and each projection is filtered by a bank of Gabor kernels (spatial frequency 0.09 cycles per pixel, four orientations, one scale of 1 pixel, kernels of $5\times5$ pixels, phase $-\pi/2$).
The responses are rectified and summed over orientations into three colour-opponent channels and a brightness channel, jointly scaled to $[0,1]$.
Because of the odd phase the filters respond to edges whatever their polarity, so the maxima of the salience lie on the contours of the objects and not inside them (Figure~\ref{fig:visual-system}b,c); a uniform region gives no response.
The salience used to choose a saccade is the mean of the colour-opponent channels; the brightness channel has weight zero in the runs reported here, so a spot that differs from its background only in brightness, such as a white spot on a black retina, is invisible to the agent.
The same colour-opponent channels, multiplied by a gain of $10^4$, form the fovea.

\paragraph{Attentional filtering.}
The salience map is divided by its maximum, with a small offset (1\%) so that a blank retina gives a uniform map, and multiplied by a Gaussian attentional mask centred on a retinal point $c$ (Figure~\ref{fig:visual-system}d,e).
The point $c$ is the centre commanded by the controller: the attention prototype of the winning location, or a random point during exploration.
The mask is broad, with a standard deviation of 22 pixels for a centre at the middle of the retina and 13 pixels for a centre more than 32 pixels from it, and it is flat when no centre is commanded.
The next gaze position is the pixel where the product is maximal (it is sampled with a probability proportional to the part of each value above 99.9999\% of the maximum, which is the maximum in practice); the action is the displacement from the current gaze to this pixel.
The mask therefore filters the salience instead of fixing the target: a command selects, among the salient points, the one closest to $c$, and the executed saccade lands on a contour near the commanded centre and not on $c$ itself.
With a flat mask the saccade goes to the global maximum of the salience, which is the bottom-up behaviour that drives exploration at first.
In the champion runs the executed saccade has the direction of the commanded one (cosine 0.92 to 1.00).

\subsubsection{Learning}

Learning is offline, at the end of each epoch.
For each recorded saccade above a size threshold, the fovea two steps before is the condition, the fovea and attention centre two steps after are the effect and the action.
The three maps are updated with the loss of Section~\ref{sec:formal} (Adam), with the goal as the anchor.
A competence predictor, a logistic read-out of a Gaussian bump on the goal, is trained to output the match $\mu$ of each sample; its output is the local competence of a goal and its mean over locations is the global competence.
Competence modulates the neighbourhood width and the loss weight, so that plasticity shrinks as the maps agree.
The maps do not change during a saccade: no receptive field moves.

\subsubsection{Algorithm}

At the midpoint of each saccade the controller reads the fovea and finds the goal $g$ and its local competence $c$.
With probability $c$ it returns the attention prototype at $g$ as the new attentional centre; otherwise it returns a random centre on a ring around the retinal centre.
Exploration therefore fades as competence grows.
Three opt-in mechanisms, all part of the configuration used below, make the executed saccades faithful to the decisions and the scanpaths diverse:
\begin{itemize}
    \item goal inhibition multiplies the conditions distances by a Gaussian bump around each of the last three goals of the episode, which lengthens the scanpaths;
    \item hold fixation zeroes the gaze displacement on all steps except the decision step, so that each goal produces exactly one saccade and no salience-driven saccade undoes it;
    \item the orienting saccade adds one salience saccade with a uniform mask at the start of each episode, so that the first goal depends on the object's orientation.
\end{itemize}
At test time the learned model acts without exploration, always using the attention prototype at the winner; hold fixation makes each goal produce one saccade, and the salience is not zeroed inside the fovea.

\subsection{Simulation setup}

Reference parameters: lattice of 100 locations, anchor width 4, neighbourhood baseline 0.5, $\sigma_\mu=10$, 1000 epochs of 20 episodes of 10 saccades.
The fovea samples a retinal window of $64\times64$ pixels resized to a $16\times16$ input.
The Gabor filters of the saliency front end have a phase offset of exactly $-\pi/2$, so that edge saliency does not depend on edge polarity.
The world alternates by epoch between the triangle and the square, so each update sees one object.
The configuration (goal inhibition, hold fixation and an orienting saccade at the start of each episode) was selected by the criteria that the goals of the two objects occupy separate regions of the conditions map, that scanpaths of different rotations share no goals, and that every goal saccade is executed.
We call it the champion; the choice is provisional.
Three champion runs were trained (seeds 014142, 017320 and 022360), and the same seeds again with the effects states permuted across the batch (shuffled pairing) as a control.
Tests present each object at nine rotations (0 to 1.6 rad in steps of 0.2).
Scanpath measures: kNN purity (share of goals whose nearest goal in another test belongs to the same object), the share of test pairs with a common goal (rotations equivalent under the object's symmetry excluded), and the number of distinct goals per test.
Retinotopy is measured with a black-box spot probe, as an experimenter would map receptive fields: Gaussian spots at known positions, soft readout, a receptive-field centre per unit, and the rank correlation $\rho$ between lattice distance and receptive-field distance, with $z$ against permutations of the centres and a control with shuffled pixel layouts.
A phase-encoded probe (sweeping bars and an expanding ring) is a second, independent mapping.
Rules were fixed before the runs where stated; analyses without a prior rule are marked as post hoc.

\subsection{Behavior of the model}

\subsubsection{Aligned maps and scanpaths}

Three elements of the control loop were needed.
Without goal inhibition the first scanpaths locked onto two or three goals.
After each decision the attention mask resets to a nearly flat distribution, so the next salience-driven saccade could undo the goal saccade and the recorded effect was not the effect of the addressed saccade; hold fixation removes these reversals but makes all scanpaths start from one shared goal, which the orienting saccade removes.
In the three champion runs objects occupy separate regions of the conditions map (kNN purity 0.92 to 0.98, mean 0.95), 7.7 distinct goals are visited per test (6.1 to 9.2), 33\% of test pairs of distinct shapes share a goal (27 to 39\%), the tests start from 14 to 17 distinct first goals, competence is 0.76 to 0.89 (mean 0.84), and the executed saccade has the direction of the one the location commands (cosine 0.92 to 1.00).

The maps are trained on view content only, with no positional label, and the probe nevertheless finds retinotopy in all of them: $z>3$ in 6 of 6 maps of the three champion runs (median $\rho$ 0.83 for the conditions maps and 0.77 for the effects maps; median $z$ 48.5 and 41.9), and the phase-encoded probe agrees ($z>3$ in 6 of 6, median $\rho$ 0.75 and 0.71).

\subsubsection{A two-spot remapping paradigm without shifting fields}

\paragraph{The classic experiments.}
Predictive remapping is defined by a geometric relation.
A neuron has a receptive field at a fixed retinal place $c$, its current field.
When the eye makes a saccade of vector $s$, a stimulus that is at retinal place $x$ before the saccade is at $x-s$ after it, so the place that will fall on the receptive field is $c+s$ in the pre-saccadic frame: the future field.
In the original experiments in monkey LIP the receptive field is first mapped during fixation; then, in a saccade task, a stimulus is flashed briefly just before the saccade at the place that the saccade will bring into the receptive field.
The neuron responds although the stimulus is outside its current field at that moment, and it does not respond to the same stimulus in a fixation control with no saccade; it also responds to the trace of a stimulus extinguished before the saccade \citep{duhamelUpdatingRepresentationVisual1992,colbySpaceAttentionParietal1999}.
The effect has been found in V3A, V3 and V2 and is rare in V1 \citep{nakamuraUpdatingVisualRepresentation2002}, and in FEF, where later data showed receptive fields converging on the saccade target \citep{umenoSpatialProcessingMonkey1997,zirnsakVisualSpaceCompressed2014}.
The most explicit measurement in space and time is that of \citet{neupaneTwoDistinctTypes2016} in V4.
Monkeys make visually guided horizontal saccades from a fixation point to a continuously lit target, towards or away from the hemifield of the receptive field, while brief probes are flashed at random places of a $10\times10$ grid spanning $40^{\circ}$: one during fixation (P1), one just before the saccade (P2) and one after it (P3).
The centre of the receptive field is the centre of mass of the responses over the grid, estimated at successive times after the probe: the current field is seen at about 75 ms, and a shifted field at 150 to 300 ms.
The shift is called future-field (FF) remapping if the vector from the first to the later centre is within $20^{\circ}$ of the saccade direction, and saccade-target (ST) remapping if it points to the target, and neurons are classified by type.
Three elements make these experiments tests of prediction and not of vision: the saccade vector is set relative to the receptive field, the probe is flashed before the saccade, and the response is compared with a fixation control and followed in time.

\paragraph{What the simulation shares with them.}
The simulation keeps the stimulus sequence and the logic of the mapping, and replaces the neuron by a location of the lattice.
A first spot $F$ at the fixation point and a second spot $T$ at the target define the saccade vector $v=T-F$, as the fixation point and the target define $s$.
A probe is flashed at the places of a grid covering the retina, as in the probe grid, and the receptive field of a face of the location is read from the responses, as the receptive field of the neuron is read from the responses over the grid.
The geometry is that of the experiments: before the saccade $F$ is at the centre of the retina and $T$ at $v$; after it $T$ is at the centre and $F$ at $-v$, so each stimulus is displaced by $-v$ on the retina.
Chance is estimated by shuffling the saccade vectors across displays, which plays the role of the comparison with chance of the experiments.
The correspondence is therefore: fixation point, $F$; saccade target, $T$; saccade vector, $v$; probe grid, lone probes on a grid; receptive field of the neuron, receptive field of a face of the recruited location; classification of neurons, a statistic compared with shuffled saccade vectors.

\paragraph{The simulated paradigm.}
(1) A spot $F$ is shown at the centre of the retina and a spot $T$ at an offset $v$ from it.
Both are red Gaussian blobs (sigma 3 pixels) on the white retina, drawn through the Gabor front end like the objects of training (Section~\ref{sec:visual-system}).
$T$ is drawn from a probability map, the mean of the conditions prototypes of the run read as a map over the fovea; offsets under 16 pixels from $F$ are redrawn.
(2) The view with both spots recruits a location of the lattice: the conditions prototype nearest to it wins and a Gaussian on the lattice around the winner spreads its activity to the neighbouring units.
(3) A lone probe spot is flashed at the places of a grid covering the retina in steps of 4 pixels.
Presented to a map, the probe is won by one unit; the recruited location responds as far as that unit lies in its lattice neighbourhood, so the probe drives the location only inside the receptive field of the face that is read.
Probes more than 28 pixels from the gaze are left out, because a spot near the edge of the 64-pixel window leaves an almost blank view.
The response of the location at a place is expressed as its percentile rank among all the probed places of the map (1 is the strongest, 0.5 a random place).
(4) Displays enter the statistics if both maps respond somewhere and $|v|$ is at most 28 pixels on each axis.
The mean percentile is computed over the testable displays with a 95\% interval over the recruited cells, since displays share cells; for the places that depend on $v$, chance is the same mean with the saccade vectors shuffled across displays (500 shuffles).
The readout was checked on hand-built maps whose receptive fields lie at known places: it recovers them within 1 pixel, and with spots planted at $0$, $v$ and $-v$ the percentile at the planted place is 0.99 against 0.72 for shuffled saccade vectors.

\paragraph{The narrative: the attentional filter and the two faces.}
In the model the saccade is chosen by a Gaussian attentional filter that multiplies the salience map (Section~\ref{sec:visual-system}).
Before the saccade the filter is centred on the fixation point $F$: this is the current attentional field, and the view at that moment, with $F$ at the centre and $T$ at $v$, is what the conditions prototype of the recruited location holds.
The saccade is the movement of the filter to $T$; after it $T$ is at the centre and $F$ at $-v$, and the field has changed: this view is what the effects prototype of the same location holds.
The two faces are therefore the same location seen with the filter on $F$ and with the filter on $T$, and the effects face is pre-activated because the filter has not yet moved to $T$.
This gives four expected places (Figure~\ref{fig:twospot-trial}).
The conditions face should respond at $F$ and at $T$ before the shift, and the effects face at $T$ and at $F$ after it.
The place $0$ (the place of $F$ before the shift and of $T$ after it) is shared by the prototypes of all locations, so a lone probe there is won by one arbitrary unit and the place is not selective; the informative places are $v$ for the conditions face and $-v$ for the effects face, and the conditions face at $-v$ and the effects face at $v$ are the specificity checks.
The effects face responding at $-v$ is the model's version of the anticipation of the classic experiments: before the filter moves, the location already responds at the place that the first spot, the stimulus that now holds attention, will have after the move.

\paragraph{Results.}
The two spots recruit 18 to 23 of the 100 locations per run, and the recruited location holds a saccade 13 to 15 pixels from the displayed one, so recruitment by the two-blob view is rough.
The conditions face responds at the place of the target before the shift in all three runs (rank 0.88 to 0.91, above the shuffled value, $p=0.002$); this is the least informative result, since the location was recruited by a view that contains $T$.
The effects face responds at the place where the first spot will be after the shift in two runs (rank 0.69 and 0.81, against 0.51 and 0.65 for shuffled vectors, $p=0.002$) and shows the dissociation expected of the narrative: the conditions face is low at $-v$ (0.38 and 0.28) and the effects face is low at $v$ (0.35 and 0.31).
The contrast between the two faces at $-v$ is clearly positive in these runs (0.31 and 0.53) and not in the third (0.05); in the third run the effects face is above 0.5 at $-v$ (0.61) but not above its shuffled value (0.60), so the anticipation of the first spot is not selective there.
The test of the classic relation fails.
If the receptive field of the effects face were the future field of the conditions field, its centre would lie at the landing of the centre of the conditions field; its displacement from the conditions centre was no closer to $-v$ than chance (error 21 to 23 pixels against 22 to 25 pixels for shuffled vectors), and classifying the displays, in earlier versions of the readout, by the nearest of the future field, the saccade target and the current field gave a future-field share of 0.00 to 0.13.
The centres of the two fields give the same picture as the ranks: the centre of the conditions field is 7 to 9 pixels from $T$, against 22 to 27 pixels for chance, and the centre of the effects field is 11 to 19 pixels from the new place of $F$ in two runs, against 25 to 32 pixels.

\paragraph{Differences from the classic experiments.}
No receptive field shifts in the model: the three maps are fixed during the saccade, and the two receptive fields compared are those of two maps, the conditions map and the effects map, not two states of one neuron.
There is no time axis, so the model has no probe flashed just before the saccade, no fixation control, no latency and no time course, and the classic test (a response at $c+s$ that is absent in fixation and appears over time) cannot be run.
The unit is not fixed: in the experiments the neuron and its field are given and the saccade is set relative to them, whereas here the displayed spots select the location, and the relation between the field and the saccade is tested afterwards against shuffled vectors.
Recruitment by two blobs is rough, the stimuli differ from the training stimuli (a spot of colour on white, since the front end is colour-opponent and a spot of white light on black gives a blank fovea), the probe is a static lone spot read by a winner-based map, and the statistics rest on three runs whose displays share 18 to 23 locations and are not independent, whereas the experiments count tens to hundreds of independent neurons.
The result is therefore not predictive remapping in the physiological sense, and it is not offered as such.

\paragraph{Link to the hypothesis.}
The central hypothesis of the paper is that a location of the lattice is one location with three faces, a condition, an action and an effect, learned from paired saccades.
Within it the remapping-like behaviour is a consequence of the pairing and not of a moving field.
The effects face holds the expected view after the saccade, including the new place of the stimulus that holds attention; because the location is recruited by the view before the saccade, that expected view is available in advance, which is what the response of the effects face at $-v$ shows.
A shift of a receptive field is then the physiological reading of a pairing that is fixed: the same two faces, read at different times, would look like a field that moves.
The simulation supports the pairing in two of three runs, as a moderate, selective effect that does not hold in the third.
The supplementary analysis (Section~\ref{sec:supp-pairing}) tests the pairing directly with single spots and a control trained with shuffled pairs.
What would turn the analogy into a test of a shifting field is a time axis: the two faces weighted over time around the saccade, with a fixation control and the classification of the shifts into future-field and saccade-target types as in V4.

\subsubsection{Stability}

The stable-perception test, foveating a rewarded part of the object from different starting views, has not been run.
A first measure, the invariance of the scanpath to the offset of the starting position of the retina, was 0.78 for the model against 0.91 for a control driven by salience alone, so the rule fixed beforehand failed and stability across starting views is not shown.
The model so far supports the learned relation between a view, a saccade and its effect, not stability as defined by the test.

\section{Discussion}

TA is not limited to the visual attention - oculomotor domain

TA is a general structure at least in fronto-parietal domains

TA is ideomotor anticipation of effects -- goals

TA in fromto-parietal loops produces neural representations which are grounded

\paragraph{Evidence and limits of the alternative to receptive-field shifting.}
In a two-spot paradigm run on the trained maps, with the attentional filter first on the fixation spot and then on the target, the conditions face of the recruited location responds at the place of the target before the shift and, in two of three runs, the effects face responds at the place where the first spot will be after the shift, selectively (percentile rank 0.69 and 0.81 against 0.51 and 0.65 for shuffled saccade vectors); the third run shows no selective response.
A single-spot analysis in the supplementary material shows that the effects face of a location follows the landing of the stimulus that activates its condition, and that this depends on the pairing in training in two of three runs.
The effect is partial.
This may reflect the capacity of the model, namely the number of units of the topology and the near-linear decoder from a location to a retinal prototype, which limit how sharply a location can pre-activate a retinal region; this has not been separated from the mechanism itself.
The time course, latency and response magnitude of remapping are abstracted away and are not addressed by the model.
The model shows that remapping signatures can arise without shifting receptive fields; it does not show that the nervous system works this way.

\subsection{Supplementary analysis: learned pairing of conditions and effects}
\label{sec:supp-pairing}

The central hypothesis is that a location of the lattice binds a condition, an action and its effect: training on paired saccades should tune the effects face of a location to where the stimulus that activates its condition lands after the saccade.
We tested this relation directly, with single spots, in the three runs with the $64\times64$ window.

\paragraph{Method.}
A spot is shown at each place $p$ of a grid over the fovea; the conditions map picks the activated location $j$, and the saccade $d(j)$ stored in it takes the spot to the landing place $q=p-d(j)$.
The effects field of $j$ is the centre of the region of the fovea where a spot activates its effects unit (spot width 3, peak read-out 0.5).
The statistic is the partial correlation, over the places whose landing is inside the fovea, between the effects field and $q$ after removing from each the part that is linear in $p$, averaged over the two coordinates, against a permutation of the effects units over locations.
As a control the effects states were permuted across the training batch (shuffled pairing), so that the condition and the effect of a location no longer come from the same view.

\paragraph{Results.}
The partial correlation is 0.72, 0.84 and 0.55 in the three runs (permutation $p=0.003$, the smallest value possible with 300 permutations).
With the shuffled pairing it falls to $-0.71$, $-0.05$ and 0.29, and the full-size shift between the conditions and the effects fields (regression ratio 0.26, 0.46 and 0.28, median 0.28) falls to a median of 0.01.
In two of the three runs the relation therefore depends on the pairing in training and not on the lattice or the read-out; in the third a part of it remains (0.29), so the pairing is not the only source in every run.
The scanpaths of the shuffled runs show the same dependence (same tests): objects are less separated on the conditions map (kNN purity 0.81 on average, range 0.62 to 0.91, against 0.95), competence is lower (0.57 against 0.84), and the effects prototype of the goal predicts the view after the saccade better than the conditions prototype in 64\% of the saccades instead of 89\%, with a median rank of 61 instead of 20 among the hundred units, while the executed saccade still follows the commanded one in two runs (cosine 0.97 and 0.98, amplitude 27 and 28 pixels against 29 and 27 pixels in the normal runs).
The run that keeps part of the relation after shuffling (022360) executes short saccades (11 pixels against 12 pixels commanded and 22 pixels in the normal run, cosine 0.73) and has competence 0.38.

\paragraph{Limits.}
The relation is partial: the field centre stays 3 to 4 fovea pixels (about 12 to 16 retinal pixels) from the landing place, and the absolute distance to the landing is only slightly smaller than for permuted units (3.8 against 4.2 fovea pixels in the run of Figure~\ref{fig:landing-example}); what the partial correlation shows is that the effects field varies with the landing beyond what the position alone predicts, not that it sits at the landing.
In the two-spot paradigm of the main text the effects receptive field was not found at the landing of the conditions receptive field, so this relation should be read as a pairing between the faces of a location, not as a displacement of a field.
A test borrowed from the physiology of remapping, the share of locations whose pre-saccadic response lies at the predicted future field, did not separate the trained maps from a permutation control (median share 0.74 against 0.69) and is not used here.
Only three runs were analysed, and one keeps part of the relation after shuffling.
